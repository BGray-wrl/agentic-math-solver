1. Introduction
Benchmarks for AI mathematical reasoning increasingly include problems whose solutions are full natural-language proofs rather than short final answers. Scoring these solutions is a bottleneck. Formal verification via proof assistants like Lean [Moura & Ullrich 2021] offers trustworthy correctness guarantees and has reached IMO silver-medal performance [AlphaProof, Nature 2025], but coverage remains limited: general-purpose models lag specialized formal provers substantially, and current pipelines typically require manual statement translation [Dekoninck et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2026]. The 2025 IMO gold-medal results from Gemini Deep Think and OpenAI's reasoning model were produced and graded in natural language [DeepMind 2025], and most current proof benchmarks ship with NL solutions only. For the foreseeable future, evaluating most AI-generated mathematics will require natural-language judging.
LLM-as-a-judge [Zheng et al. 2023] has become the standard tool, but is known to suffer from position, verbosity, and self-preference biases, and to be highly sensitive to prompt design [Gu et al. 2024]. For proofs specifically, Petrov et al. (2025) highlighted that frontier models struggle to produce valid proofs even when they reach correct final answers, motivating dedicated grading benchmarks. Dekoninck et al. (2025) extended this with the 5,000-proof Open Proof Corpus, and Luong et al. (2025) released IMO-Bench, including the 1,000-instance IMO-GradingBench we use here.
Judge cost has received less attention. While the generator can vary on a per-experiment basis, the judge must be both reliable and held fixed across an entire study, which makes its cost a tax on the whole research effort. Verga et al. (2024) proposed Panels of LLM Evaluators (PoLL), reporting that small-model panels outperform a single large judge while being ~7× cheaper on QA and chatbot-arena tasks. This motivates a concrete question: is there a cheap judge a budget-constrained researcher can trust for proof grading?
Our prior expectation was that a PoLL-style consensus would be the safest choice, as diverse models with offsetting biases should cancel each other's errors. We tested this. The consensus did perform well, but it did not outperform the best individual model inside it. The more useful finding is that the cheap tier as a whole is competitive with the frontier: in our setup, cheap open-weight judges match frontier baselines on pass/fail agreement at roughly 100× lower cost.
This places the work alongside two concurrent papers. Ma et al. (2026) systematically search the evaluator design space and show that combining a strong reasoning backbone with reference solutions, marking schemes, and ensembling reaches expert-level agreement on a fine-grained scale; we ask whether the backbone itself needs to be strong, and find that for reference-based pass/fail grading it does not. Naik et al. (2026) ask the closest version of our question in the reference-free setting and report that smaller models trail frontier judges by ~10% in accuracy and ~25% in self-consistency, with prompt ensembling closing much of the gap. Our results are complementary: in reference-based grading (against a ground-truth solution), the accuracy gap closes entirely, consistent with the intuition that the solution does work the model would otherwise have to do. Self-consistency was not measured here and remains an open question.
We are deliberately narrow about scope. This is a study of judging, not solving: we measure how well a model grades proofs against ground truth and human scores. It concerns problems with (1) a candidate proof, (2) a ground-truth proof, and (3) an expert human grade.
